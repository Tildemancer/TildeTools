About the lexicon
=================

I'm Claude, an AI made by Anthropic, and this is my account of the lexicon in
this folder: what it is, where it came from, and what my part in it was.

The folder is the offline dictionary behind Define and Synonyms in TildeTools'
right-click menus: definitions.tsv answers Define, synonyms.tsv answers
Synonyms, and chat.txt is TildeTools' own small dictionary of chat and roleplay
words.

The credits, briefly: the aggregation is mine, and the code calls are
Tildemancer's. I aggregated this lexicon in September 2026, rebuilding it some
sixty times along the way. Tildemancer made the calls, decided the filters the
plugin draws from, read everything I produced, reworked a good deal of it by
hand to fit their vision, and tested the results extensively in game.
Tildemancer sat with me throughout: none of it ran unattended, and they
contributed to the work as it happened, in between working on other parts of
the plugin.


Not AI generated, but AI-aggregated
-----------------------------------

Not one definition or synonym in the two .tsv files was generated by an AI. They
come from two sources made by people:

  - Wiktionary, written by its contributors, https://en.wiktionary.org,
    through kaikki.org's wiktextract extract of 25 September 2026
  - Open English WordNet 2025, derived from Princeton WordNet,
    https://github.com/globalwordnet/english-wordnet

What TildeTools ships is AI-aggregated: I aggregated those two sources into
something Tildemancer could use, they decided the filters the plugin draws from,
and I checked each version that came out. The text of every definition and
synonym is the source's own, and so is every "(slang)" or "(vulgar)" on one:
those labels come from the sources' own tags, bar one word closed by hand (see
Marked senses).

The one exception is chat.txt, TildeTools' own short list of chat and roleplay
words. I wrote its meanings, as a stopgap until Tildemancer can write them
themselves.


How it was made
---------------

The two .tsv files are far too big to write or check by hand, which is where I
came in: I aggregated them from Wiktionary and WordNet. I could take in far more
of the text at a time than Tildemancer could, so I raised the logic errors I
suspected and suggested filters — often as general rules, sometimes when they
came back to brainstorm how to filter the text. Many of the filters described
below started that way. Tildemancer decided which went in and tested each one,
and I checked what came out.

The same went for Define's trips online: I advised on the rules for when it
should look a word up on Wiktionary, and Tildemancer decided them.


definitions.tsv: 240,070 words
------------------------------

This holds every English entry in Wiktionary (from kaikki.org's wiktextract
extract of 25 September 2026) whose word TildeTools would accept anyway. That
means a word in either spelling list, a word from the game's own text, a
spelling in chat.txt, or a one-word entry in Open English WordNet. A hyphenated
word counts when each of its halves does, and so does any word that shares its
line with a political one, for the reason given under Real-world politics.

Why filter at all? All of English Wiktionary is many times larger, and a word
the spellchecker would underline is most likely a typo, not something anyone
wants defined. Anything outside the filter falls back to Wiktionary online, if
"Look a word up online" is on in the Spelling tab. WordNet's one-word entries
went in because they're real English that the spelling lists leave out: 13,266
more words, "callipygous" among them.

Each sense keeps only its last gloss. Wiktionary nests its senses, and every
nested one repeats its parents' glosses before its own, so the last gloss is the
most specific — and the only one that isn't a repeat.

A form points to its base word: in the Define window, "plural of sabaton" is
followed by sabaton's own senses. Different capitalisations of a word share one
line, lowercase first, so "paris" comes before "Paris", and the window gives
each its own tab.

One bug deserves a place on the record, precisely because nothing complained
about it. The first build Tildemancer shared with me found each entry's word
with a plain text search for "word", which could match a nested record's word
before the entry's own. Happy's adjective ended up filed under "hepi" — which
appears 6,605 characters earlier in its record — and was thrown away as unknown.
Parsing each record whole fixed it, and brought back 17,678 senses and 258
words.


synonyms.tsv: 106,581 words
---------------------------

The synonyms come from Open English WordNet 2025. Each word gets one group per
sense, listing the other words that share that sense, with the lowercase word's
senses first; after those comes one group of anything Wiktionary lists as a
synonym that WordNet didn't already give. Duplicate groups are dropped. A word
never appears in its own list, and neither does a synonym holding the word or a
form of it — marching and marching music aren't offered for march, nor killing
for kill. That takes out 12,678, most of them phrases holding the word itself,
which the menus already skipped.

The groups are ranked, because the right-click menus show only the first ten.
Plain groups come first, WordNet's and Wiktionary's together; then groups in a
slang or derogatory sense; then closed ones; and last of all, the synonyms of a
headword in another case, which is why Bush's nicknames sit behind bush's. Among
the plain groups the commonest words lead, measured by the sizes of SCOWL, the
list en_US comes from, where 35 is everyday English and 85 the rarest it keeps.
A word goes by its size, and a word SCOWL doesn't list goes after every word it
does; a phrase goes by its rarest word's size, and a group by its words'
average. Within a group, plain words come before labelled ones.

Two words show what the ranking does. Tildemancer found "fast" offering
debauched, degenerate and libertine first: WordNet has no synonyms for fast as
speed, and of the senses that have some, it lists the dissolute one first. Now
the menu leads with flying, quick and firm. Before any ranking, "screw" gave
prison guards and sex terms, because WordNet lists the prison-guard slang first
and gives the fastener no synonyms at all. Now it leads with drive in, affix and
assemble. For a lowercase word, the menus also skip capitalised synonyms, which
name something else.

Two more mistakes turned up in review. WordNet files a word's capitalised senses
first, so "march" offered Master of Architecture before marching, and "polish"
offered Polish language first. And Wiktionary's synonym lists carry headings,
notes and links alongside the words, which the build Tildemancer shared with me
kept as if they were words: 432 of them, "Intransitive:" for accustom and
"mul:Hg" for mercury among them — every one something you could have picked from
the menu. The build now leaves out anything with a colon, which costs midnight
its "00:00" and "24:00". Two headings without one slipped through that net for a
while, "Formal terms" and "Informal and slang terms", 38 times each, until
Tildemancer's screenshot of "screw" showed them; they're left out by name now.


Marked senses
-------------

Tildemancer looked up "garden" and was offered "pubic hair". That's correct —
it's Wiktionary's slang sense of garden — but startling, with nothing to say
why. So every sense now carries the strongest label its source gives it, out of
slur, offensive, vulgar, derogatory and slang: "(slang)" before its gloss, and
after each synonym it brings. 12,699 of 508,787 senses are labelled (278 slur,
491 offensive, 1,225 vulgar, 2,054 derogatory, 8,651 slang), along with 85,106
of 613,665 synonyms.

Slurs, offensive and vulgar senses stay out of sight until someone asks for
them. Tildemancer's solution was a closed section: the Define window keeps them
under a closed "Potentially NSFW" line at the end of a word's senses and of its
synonyms, and the right-click menus leave them out. Nothing is taken away, and
nothing like that turns up unasked. They chose closed over gone for slurs too,
rather than leaving them out of the files — which also means a slur looked up on
purpose gets that closed line instead of falling through to Wiktionary online.

Behind that line, synonyms show without their labels. A slur sense's list in
Wiktionary often has respectful words in it, "person of color" and "Caucasian"
among them, and in the files they inherit the sense's label. Showing them as
"(slur)" would call them slurs, so the window doesn't; the closed line says
enough.

Where the labels come from:

  - Wiktionary's own tags, on a sense or on one synonym. Wiktionary tags most
    slurs only "offensive", which is why offensive is closed along with slur. A
    few senses call themselves an ethnic slur only in the gloss; those count
    too.
  - WordNet's usage domains. A group filed under ethnic slur is all slurs, and
    one filed under obscenity is vulgar throughout — it's that sense of the
    word, so under "screw" the sex groups close whole. In a group filed under
    disparagement or slang, each word shows the label Wiktionary gives that
    word, so under "idiot", imbecile is derogatory and half-wit is plain. The
    first version Tildemancer shared with me labelled every word in those groups
    too, and "make love (vulgar)" read as badly as it sounds.
  - A WordNet group that a vulgar sense's definition opens with is that sense:
    bush's "A person's pubic hair" makes "pubic hair, crotch hair" vulgar under
    bush. The definition has to open with it, or doughnut's anus "with the
    outward shape of a donut" would take the pastry along. Only vulgar senses do
    this, because an offensive sense's definition names the people it insults,
    and those words are the respectful ones.
  - A labelled synonym whose word has a closed sense somewhere is taken in that
    sense. An ableist slur listed under "idiot" is closed; the same word listed
    under "delay" isn't.
  - A form of a word whose every sense is closed, a "plural of", is closed too.
  - And by hand, one word the sources leave untagged: slutty, which Wiktionary
    lists under fast with no sense and tags only informal. It's closed as
    offensive, the tag fast's promiscuous sense carries.

A label describes the sense it was found on, not the synonym on its own: pubic
hair isn't slang, but that meaning of garden is. Choosing a synonym, from the
menu or in the window, drops the label before it's used or looked up.


Real-world politics
-------------------

None of it turns up unasked. Tildemancer found GW and GWB, George W. Bush's
initials, among the synonyms for bush, and pointed out that nobody playing FFXIV
is talking about him. So a Wiktionary sense is left out if it's filed under a
country's politics ("US politics", "UK politics"), if it's a nickname for a real
person, if it's a name defined by a political office ("George W. Bush, 43rd
president of the United States"), or if it's a derogatory political sense. So is
anything filed under one of the twelve politicians those nicknames belong to —
which is how GW and GWB leave bush, since Wiktionary files them under George W.
Bush and nothing else.

An acronym keeps its own politics. Typing GWB, POTUS or DNC is asking what it
stands for, and Tildemancer's call was that an intentional political acronym, or
a possibly intentional one, can stay: GWB keeps George W. Bush beside the George
Washington Bridge. None of them turns up as another word's synonym.

All told, that's 2,615 senses left out, and 1,717 words that had nothing else. A
word keeps its other senses — blue is still a colour, and only its four party
senses are gone — and the spellchecker still accepts every one of those words;
they just have no definition here. Any word that shares a line with a political
one is written out, whether the spellchecker knows it or not, so tea party is
still tea, and Obama is still a surname and a city in Fukui. A line that
politics left with nothing stays in the file, empty — 1,577 of them — and the
Define window takes an empty line to mean "left out". It says so, rather than
fetching the word from Wiktionary online, where the politics would come straight
back.

The rule is blunt. A nickname for anyone real goes, so Satchmo leaves with
Dubya, and so does shogun, which Wiktionary files under Japanese politics. I
suggested to Tildemancer that a nickname could go through when nothing else
marked it political. On implementation and review, Killary and Slick Willie
came back, and Tildemancer decided to keep it blunt; the call is theirs. WordNet
2025 holds no named people at all, so there was nothing to take out of it.


Reading the files
-----------------

Each .tsv file has one word per line: the word in lowercase, a tab, then its
items, separated by the unit separator, U+001F. A definition item is
"[Headword ]part of speech: [(mark) ]gloss", and the headword is only there
when its capitalisation differs from the key. A form adds the record separator,
U+001E, and its base word. A synonym item is one group, with its words
separated by ", " and each followed by " (mark)" where one applies. A word left
out as politics has nothing after its tab.

The lines are sorted by the ordinal order of the decoded text, and that matters
more than it looks: the plugin never loads these files. It binary-searches them
on disk each time you look a word up, so they cost no memory until they're
used — and if they were sorted by raw UTF-8 bytes instead, the search would go
wrong for words with characters beyond the Basic Multilingual Plane. So rebuild
them rather than editing them by hand.


chat.txt
--------

TildeTools' own words, whose meanings I wrote; the file's header explains its
format. Its spellings are accepted in any case but never offered as corrections,
so a typo like "lok" is never "fixed" into "lol". Its meanings show first in
Define, ahead of Wiktionary's.
